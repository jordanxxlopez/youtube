# Episode 1 — science notes

**Working title:** The Brain Team Challenge  
**Checked:** November 18, 2026  
**Scope:** Shared science for the 2D and 3D versions; a teaching tour, not a clinical or exhaustive brain model.

## Core lesson

The cerebrum has two hemispheres. Each is commonly described using four main
lobes: frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital. This episode introduces those
lobes, while explaining that most activities involve cooperating brain regions.
The four stops are the order of our tour, not the order in which the brain must
process everything. The cerebellum and brainstem are also parts of the brain.
[Source: Queensland Brain Institute](https://qbi.uq.edu.au/brain/brain-anatomy/lobes-brain)

## Child-friendly wording

| Topic | Suitable dialogue | Accuracy boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Occipital lobe | “This area helps your brain make sense of what your eyes see.” | Eyes collect visual information; seeing also involves other regions. |
| Temporal lobe | “Parts of this lobe help us understand sounds, including Dani's voice.” | Hearing is one example; temporal regions also contribute to language and memory. Hearing is not required to catch a ball. |
| Parietal lobe | “This area helps put together touch and information about where things are around us.” | Do not label it merely the ‘touch button.’ Spatial processing combines information. |
| Frontal lobe | “Parts of this lobe help us plan, and other parts help direct movements.” | No single lobe is the brain's all-purpose boss. |

These are representative contributions, not exclusive jobs or a complete list
of functions. [Source: Queensland Brain Institute](https://qbi.uq.edu.au/brain/brain-anatomy/lobes-brain)

## The ball-catching challenge

Use the catch as an illustration of cooperation: visual information helps
track the ball; sensory information helps locate the hands and ball; motor
systems help produce the movement. The cerebellum helps coordinate movement
and its timing, and contributes to learning through practice. Show it tucked behind the brainstem and below the back of the
cerebrum, rather than calling it a fifth cerebral lobe.
[Source: UTHealth Neuroscience Online, Cerebellum](https://nba.uth.tmc.edu/neuroscience/m/s3/chapter05.html)

Include the rest of the body: signals travel through the spinal cord and
peripheral nerves to muscles, and sensory information travels back. A simple
screen can show “brain ↔ spinal cord ↔ nerves ↔ body,” while explaining that
this is a simplified overview. Do not show one magical wire straight from a
lobe to the hand. The nervous system consists of communicating networks.
[Source: NICHD](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/neuro/conditioninfo/parts)

The specific ball-catching story is a teaching application of these general
functions, not a claim that the sources establish a fixed four-lobe sequence.

## Cell guides

- **Neuron guide:** A neuron is a nerve cell. Typical neurons have a cell body,
  branching dendrites, and an axon. Many receive inputs through dendrites and
  send electrical signals along an axon; chemical messengers transmit signals
  at many synapses. Glowing trails are a visual analogy, not visible light in
  living tissue. Avoid claiming that all neurons have identical shapes or
  that every synapse works the same way.
- **Astrocyte guide:** Astrocytes help maintain the environment around neurons
  and support their function. A brief line can be “I help keep conditions right
  for our neuron neighbors.” Branching processes are appropriate; a uniform
  five-point star is a mascot convention, not the defining anatomy.
- **Microglia guide:** Microglia contribute to brain immune defense and can
  engulf cellular debris. A line such as “I help clear away bits of damaged
  cells” is suitable. They do not sweep electrical messages away, remove
  thoughts, or instantly fix a traffic jam in a signal path.

[Sources: Society for Neuroscience / BrainFacts](https://www.brainfacts.org/brain-anatomy-and-function/cells-and-circuits/2022/neurons-and-glia-113022)
and [UTHealth Neuroscience Online, Chapter 8](https://nba.uth.tmc.edu/neuroscience/m/s1/chapter08.html).

## Visual and story checks

- Establish that the room, enlarged cells, trails, and talking guides belong
  to Dani's simulation. Real cells do not have eyes, voices, or intentions.
- Label a side-view brain consistently: frontal toward the face, occipital at
  the back, temporal along the lower side, parietal above it and behind the
  frontal region. Match all labels to the same orientation.
- If a side view highlights one hemisphere, state that the other hemisphere
  has these main lobes too. Do not imply the four named lobes make up the
  entire brain.
- Use overlapping network activity during the final catch. Do not animate
  the four lobe colors turning on in a compulsory one-at-a-time relay.
- Keep the catch gentle and optional; viewers can watch or imagine it. Avoid
  treating successful catching as a test of intelligence or brain health.
- Do not add fixed neuron/glia population counts; they are unnecessary here.
- The six questions should test what the scene just showed, with time to
  respond and a supportive answer. Questions about sound must also have a
  visible/captioned cue.
